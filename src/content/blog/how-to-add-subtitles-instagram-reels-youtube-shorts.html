Both platforms can caption your short videos automatically, but they handle captions differently. YouTube Shorts support proper closed-caption tracks, including ones you upload. Instagram Reels offer automatic closed captions, and for anything beyond that, the usual answer is to burn the text into the video before you post. If you publish the same clip on both, you can correct the captions once and use them twice.

Closed captions or burned-in text?

Closed captions are a separate track that viewers can switch on and off. The platform draws them, so you don't control the font or position, but they can be edited after publishing and don't clutter the frame for people who don't want them.

Burned-in (open) captions are part of the picture. They're always visible, look the same everywhere, and can match your style. But they can't be turned off or corrected without re-uploading. The open vs closed captions article goes into more detail.

One thing to avoid is having both on the same video. If you burn captions in and the platform also shows closed captions, viewers who have captions switched on see the same words twice, one set on top of the other.

YouTube Shorts: your options

Automatic captions. YouTube generates them for Shorts in supported languages, the same as for long videos. YouTube's automatic captions help page says they may not be ready right after upload, that processing time depends on the audio, and that you should always review them. It also lists reasons they may never appear: an unsupported language, poor sound, a long silence at the start, or overlapping speakers.

Captions in the Shorts editor. In the YouTube app on iPhone and iPad, the Shorts editor has a Captions option that adds automatic captions. You can change the font and text colour and correct mistakes. YouTube notes that this is only available in certain languages and on certain devices (see Enhance your Shorts). The page doesn't say whether these captions end up in the picture or as a separate track, so if you also plan to upload a caption file, check the published Short for doubled text.

Upload a caption file. This is the most accurate route. In YouTube Studio, open Subtitles, choose the Short, add a language, then pick Upload file and With timing for an SRT. If you have plain text without timestamps, choose Without timing, or use Auto-sync and let YouTube set the timing. The steps are on YouTube's Add subtitles & captions page.

A quick way to get an accurate caption file for a Short

You don't need to type the captions from scratch. Once YouTube has created automatic captions for your Short:

  1. Paste the Short's link into FetchSubs. Shorts links work the same as normal video links, and the Shorts transcript page has the details.
  2. Download the auto-generated track as SRT.
  3. Open it in a text editor or Subtitle Edit and fix names, product names, slang and numbers. These are what speech recognition gets wrong most often.
  4. Upload the corrected file in YouTube Studio with timing, as above.

A Short only has a few minutes of speech at most, so step 3 is quick. One thing to watch: YouTube's auto captions are rolling captions, so in the SRT the cues overlap. When I downloaded the auto track of a longer NASA video, 55 of its 63 cues overlapped the next one. That doesn't matter if you upload the file back to YouTube, but it does matter if you import it into a video editor to burn in. The subtitle timing guide shows how to remove the overlaps.

Instagram Reels: your options

According to Instagram's Help Center article Manage closed captions for your reels, Instagram uses speech recognition to create closed captions for reels automatically, and you can turn them on or off. It also says captions may not be available to everyone. Instagram's menus move between app versions, so follow the current Help Center steps rather than a screenshot in a blog post (including this one).

I couldn't find any option in Instagram's help pages for uploading your own caption file to a reel. So if you want captions you've checked word by word, or a particular look, burn them into the video before uploading. Editors such as CapCut, VEED or Kapwing, and desktop editors like DaVinci Resolve and Premiere Pro, can do this, and many of them accept an SRT file as input, so you don't have to retype anything. If you're working in Resolve or Premiere, see importing SRT files into Resolve and Premiere.

If you rely on Instagram's automatic captions instead, read them before posting, and check the reel again afterwards with captions switched on.

Correct once, use on both

If the same clip goes out as a Short and a Reel, this is the order I'd use:

  1. Publish the Short first and let YouTube create its automatic captions.
  2. Download them as SRT with FetchSubs, correct them, and upload the corrected file to the Short.
  3. Import the same corrected SRT into your editor, style it, and export a burned-in version for Instagram.
  4. Post that version as the Reel, and leave Instagram's own automatic captions off so the words don't appear twice.

The catch is timing. The SRT matches the exact video it came from. If the Instagram version is trimmed differently, even by half a second at the start, every caption will be off by that amount. Shift the whole file by the difference before burning it in. If you cut the Short out of a longer video, the same applies: captions from the long video count from its start, so subtract the point where your clip begins.

Making captions readable on a phone

  • Use white or yellow text with a dark outline or a semi-transparent box behind it. Plain white text disappears against bright backgrounds.
  • Keep it to one or two short lines. A vertical frame is narrow, so a line that fits comfortably on a 16:9 video will wrap or shrink here. As a ceiling, Netflix's English style guide allows 42 characters per line for widescreen video. For vertical video I'd aim for well under that.
  • Stay away from the edges. Both apps put buttons, your username and the description over the bottom and right side of the frame. Upload a test, look at it in the feed, and move the captions up if anything covers them.
  • Check the size on an actual phone. Text that looks large in an editor on a monitor can be small in the feed.
  • Proofread before you animate. Word-by-word animated captions are popular, but a typo in big, bouncing text is hard to miss.

A final note on what FetchSubs does and doesn't do here: it downloads caption tracks that already exist on YouTube, as SRT, VTT or plain text. It doesn't create captions for a video that has none, and it can't download anything from Instagram. For Reels, it's only useful when the same video is also on YouTube.
