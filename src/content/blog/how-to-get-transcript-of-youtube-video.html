--- title: "How to Get the Transcript of a YouTube Video (Desktop, iPhone & Android)" summary: "Open a YouTube transcript on desktop, iPhone or Android, what to do when Show transcript is missing, and how to save it as a TXT or SRT file." author: "Halit Burak Eroğlu" publishDate: "2026-11-22" lang: "en" order: 30 ---

Open the video, expand the description and click or tap Show transcript. That opens a panel with every caption line, and the line being spoken is highlighted as the video plays. It works in a desktop browser and in the YouTube app on iPhone, iPad and Android.

What YouTube doesn't give you is a way to download that transcript. If you want to keep the text, paste the video link into a caption downloader such as FetchSubs and save it as plain text (TXT) or with timestamps (SRT or VTT). The steps for each device are below, followed by the usual reasons the button is missing and a real example of what the saved file looks like.

On a computer

  1. Open the video on youtube.com in Chrome, Edge, Firefox or Safari.
  2. Click the description box under the video (or its ...more link) to expand it.
  3. Scroll to the end of the description and click Show transcript. On many videos it sits in a small Transcript section near the bottom.
  4. The transcript opens to the right of the player, or below it if the window is narrow. Click any line to jump to that moment.

At the top of the panel there is normally a three-dot menu (⋮) with a Toggle timestamps option. Use it before you select anything: with the times hidden you can read, or drag-select, just the words. If the video has captions in more than one language, a language selector usually sits at the bottom of the panel, and machine captions are labelled there, for example English (auto-generated). On videos with chapters, the chapter titles show up as headings inside the transcript.

YouTube moves things around now and then and tests different layouts on different accounts, so your button or menu may not be exactly where I describe it. Before you give up, expand the description fully and scroll all the way down. Google's own short description of the feature is on its View video transcripts help page.

On iPhone or iPad

  1. Open the video in the YouTube app.
  2. Tap the video title, or ...more under the player, to open the full description.
  3. Scroll down and tap Show transcript.
  4. The transcript replaces the description sheet. Tap a line to jump to that point.

Use the app for this. The mobile website (m.youtube.com in Safari) often has no Show transcript button at all, so if you're in a browser and can't find it, open the same video in the app or request the desktop site. And don't expect to copy anything: the app shows the transcript but doesn't let you select the text in any useful way.

On Android

Same steps as on iPhone: open the video in the app, tap the title or ...more, scroll to the bottom of the description and tap Show transcript. On tablets and foldables the transcript may open as a side panel rather than a sheet, depending on the app version. The same limits apply too. You can read the text but not reliably copy it, and the timestamp toggle from the desktop site generally isn't there.

YouTube Shorts

The Shorts player is stripped down, and you may not find a transcript option in it. Change the address from youtube.com/shorts/VIDEO_ID to youtube.com/watch?v=VIDEO_ID and the Short opens in the normal watch page, with the usual description and, if captions exist, the Show transcript button. More on this on the Shorts transcript page.

No Show transcript button?

A transcript is just the video's caption track displayed as a list. No caption track, no transcript. The quickest check is the CC button in the player: if captions can't be switched on, there's no transcript either. That usually means the video is very new and its automatic captions haven't been processed yet, the creator turned captions off, the language isn't covered by YouTube's speech recognition, or there's little clear speech to recognise (music, gameplay, noisy recordings). If CC works but the button is missing, you're probably on the mobile website.

No tool can export a transcript YouTube doesn't have, FetchSubs included, because they all read the same caption tracks. I go through each cause and what you can do about it in YouTube transcript not available.

Where the transcript panel falls short

The panel is good for reading along or finding a quote. For getting the text out, it's clumsy. There's no download or copy button, so on desktop you click before the first line and drag to the last while the panel scrolls. On a one-hour talk that is slow and easy to get wrong. If you forget to hide the timestamps first, every line comes with its time, and chapter headings come along as well (how to copy a YouTube transcript without timestamps covers the cleanup). On a phone you can't copy at all. And you get one language at a time, with nothing saved for later.

Saving a YouTube transcript as a file

If the text is headed for a document, a notes app or an AI chat, downloading it is quicker than copying it. With FetchSubs the steps are the same on a computer and a phone:

  1. Copy the video link. On a computer, take it from the address bar. In the app, tap Share, then Copy link. Watch links, youtu.be short links, Shorts links and embed links all work.
  2. Paste it on fetchsubs.com/youtube-transcript (or the home page) and press the button. You'll see the title, the length and every caption track the video has, each marked Official (added by a person) or Auto-generated (YouTube's speech recognition).
  3. Next to the track you want, choose a format. TXT gives you the words only. SRT or VTT keep a start and end time for every line. RAW opens the timestamp-free text in a new browser tab without saving a file, which is the easiest option on a phone: long-press, Select All, Copy.

No sign-up, nothing to install. FetchSubs doesn't create captions: it doesn't transcribe audio, translate or summarise. It exports the caption tracks YouTube already has, in whatever languages the video offers.

What the downloaded file looks like

I ran Me at the zoo, the first video ever uploaded to YouTube (19 seconds), through FetchSubs. It has two caption tracks, English and German, both added by a person. Here is the start of the English track as SRT, saved as Me_at_the_zoo_en.srt:

1
00:00:01,200 --> 00:00:03,360
All right, so here we are, in front of the
elephants

2
00:00:05,318 --> 00:00:07,974
the cool thing about these guys is that they
have really...

3
00:00:07,974 --> 00:00:12,616
really really long trunks

And the same track as TXT (Me_at_the_zoo_en.txt):

All right, so here we are, in front of the
elephants
the cool thing about these guys is that they
have really...
really really long trunks
and that's cool
(baaaaaaaaaaahhh!!)
and that's pretty much all there is to
say

The words are identical; TXT simply drops the cue numbers and times. Line breaks follow how the captions were split on screen, so one sentence can run across two lines, and the captioner's sound description (the elephant) stays in. The VTT version is the SRT with a WEBVTT header on top and a dot instead of a comma before the milliseconds (00:00:01.200). If you're unsure which to pick, the SRT vs VTT vs TXT comparison explains where each one fits.

Picking the right track

Popular videos often have several tracks. Steve Jobs' 2005 Stanford commencement address, for example, lists nine in FetchSubs, among them an official English track and an auto-generated English one. Each row has its own buttons, so you get exactly the track you click; files from the automatic track end in _auto (..._en_auto.txt), which keeps the two apart in your downloads folder.

When both exist, take the official one. The difference shows from the first line. The official track of the Jobs speech opens with This program is brought to you by Stanford University., while the automatic one starts with a [Music] tag and then this program is brought to you by in lowercase, with no full stop anywhere. Automatic tracks exist on far more videos, so they're often all you have; the auto-generated subtitles accuracy test shows what to expect from them.

Let the timestamps decide the format. If you might ever need to find the moment something was said, for a citation, a fact check or a clip, download the SRT as well. You can always turn an SRT into clean text later. You can't put the times back into a TXT file.
