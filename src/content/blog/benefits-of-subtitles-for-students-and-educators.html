A transcript turns a video into something you can search, skim, quote and annotate. For a student that means faster notes and better revision; for a teacher it means checking a video in five minutes instead of fifty and handing out material that more students can actually use. This post covers the uses I think are worth the effort, and the places where captions let you down.

To get a sense of scale: I downloaded the English captions of Steve Jobs' 2005 Stanford commencement address (about 15 minutes) with FetchSubs, and the creator-uploaded track came to roughly 2,300 words. Randy Pausch's "Last Lecture" at Carnegie Mellon, 76 minutes long, came to about 12,600 words. So a typical lecture hour is somewhere around 9,000 to 10,000 words of text, which is a long chapter, not a pamphlet. That's why the techniques below are about finding and selecting, not reading everything.

For students

Turn a lecture into notes you'll actually use

Say you have a 90-minute architecture history lecture on YouTube and an exam next week. Rewatching it and pausing every minute is slow. Instead:

  1. Paste the video link into FetchSubs and download the transcript as TXT, or open RAW to read it in a browser tab.
  2. Skim it once and write down the sections in order. Lecturers announce structure out loud ("the second reason is...", "moving on to..."), and those phrases are easy to spot on the page even when they're easy to miss while listening.
  3. Search for the terms on your syllabus, say "Gothic Revival" or "Brutalism", and read the explanation around each hit.
  4. Write each explanation in your own words. Copy-pasting feels productive, but it's the rewriting that shows you whether you understood it.

Go back to the video for anything visual: slides, diagrams, buildings, experiments. The transcript only contains what was said.

Revise by testing yourself, not by re-reading

A large review of study techniques by Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) rated practice testing and spaced practice as the two most useful techniques they looked at, and rated re-reading and highlighting as low utility. A transcript makes the useful kind easy. Take a paragraph, turn its main claim into a question, and put the answer on the back of a card. A few days later, answer without looking and check against the text.

An AI assistant can draft questions from a transcript quickly. Check every one: they sometimes ask about things the lecturer never said, or turn a passing remark into a "key concept".

Quote accurately and cite the moment

If you cite a talk in an essay, the transcript gives you the exact words and the SRT file gives you the timestamp, so a reader can find the passage. Listen to the quoted part once before you hand it in. Automatic captions mishear words, and even creator tracks have typos: the official English track of the Stanford speech above spells "believing" as "Beleiveing" in one line.

Turn captions on while you watch

Captions help when a lecturer speaks fast, has an accent you're not used to, or uses a lot of technical vocabulary, and they help anyone studying in a second language. If the automatic captions spell a key term oddly, check it in your textbook before it lands in your notes.

For teachers

Preview a video before you assign it

Before assigning an hour-long documentary, skim its transcript. In a few minutes you can see whether it covers your objectives, find the part you actually want students to watch, and spot anything unsuitable for your class. The SRT timestamps then let you assign a precise section ("watch 12:40 to 21:15") instead of the whole thing. Students are more likely to watch nine focused minutes than an hour.

Make assigned videos more accessible

Giving students the transcript alongside an assigned video helps deaf and hard-of-hearing students, students with processing or attention difficulties, students using screen readers, and students working in a second language. It also helps the student with a slow connection who can't stream video on the bus. Three cautions:

  • Automatic captions may not be accurate enough to count as a formal accommodation. Prefer videos that have the creator's own subtitles (FetchSubs labels which tracks are auto-generated), and ask your institution's disability or accessibility office what it requires. Our guide to WCAG and video transcripts covers the standards side.
  • A transcript doesn't describe visuals. If the video depends on a chart or a demonstration, add a sentence or two describing what's on screen.
  • Auto-generated text is hard to read as a handout. The automatic English track of the Stanford speech has almost no punctuation and includes markers like [Music] and [Applause]. If you're giving it to students, spend ten minutes adding sentence breaks, or pick a video with a creator track.

Prepare materials faster

A transcript is a quick starting point for:

  • a glossary of the terms used in the video;
  • comprehension questions in the order the video covers them, so students can answer while watching;
  • a gap-fill exercise for language classes, made by blanking out key words in a short passage;
  • a short reading guide for students who prefer to read first and watch second.

You can write these yourself or have an AI assistant draft them from the transcript. Either way, review every item before it reaches students; a wrong answer key does more damage than no answer key.

Keep a searchable folder of the videos you reuse

If you assign the same videos every year, save their transcripts in one folder with the video link pasted at the top of each file (the TXT download contains only the spoken text, not the URL). When a student asks "which video explained osmosis?", a desktop search across the folder answers it in seconds. FetchSubs works one video at a time, so build the folder as you go rather than all at once.

Where this doesn't work

  • No captions, no transcript. FetchSubs downloads caption tracks that already exist on YouTube; it doesn't transcribe audio. Many videos have automatic captions, but some don't, especially music-heavy videos, unsupported languages and very recent uploads. See what to do when a transcript isn't available.
  • No speaker names. In a panel discussion, automatic captions don't say who is talking, so attribute quotes carefully.
  • Copyright still applies. Reading a transcript for study or discussing it in class is one thing. Reproducing a whole transcript in published or paid course materials is another; credit the creator and check the licence. See what you can and can't do with YouTube subtitles.
