--- title: "How to Turn Your Own YouTube Videos into Blog Posts" summary: "Turn your own YouTube video into a readable blog post: get the transcript, find the structure, rewrite for readers and add what the video was missing." author: "Halit Burak Eroğlu" publishDate: "2026-02-13" updatedDate: "2026-11-22" lang: "en" order: 6 ---

Every video you've published already contains a draft article: the words you said. Getting that text out takes a minute. Turning it into a post people want to read takes longer, because speech and writing work differently. This guide covers both, step by step, for videos you made yourself.

Why bother? A written version reaches people who won't sit through a 20-minute video, can be skimmed and bookmarked, can be quoted and linked by other sites, and gives search engines text to index, which a video on its own doesn't.

First: only your own videos

A transcript is a copy of what someone said, and what they said is their work. Publishing another creator's transcript without their permission is copyright infringement, even if you credit them and link to the video. An article that closely follows someone else's video, point by point in your own words, can infringe too. It's also the kind of page Google's spam policies describe under scraping: republishing content from other sites without adding original value.

So this workflow is for your own videos, or videos whose creator has given you written permission to adapt them. Quoting a short passage from someone else's video with credit and a link, in an article that is mostly your own analysis, is a different situation. Our guide to subtitle copyright explains where those lines usually fall. It isn't legal advice, and if a lot depends on it, ask a lawyer in your country.

Step 1: Get the transcript

Paste the video link into FetchSubs. You'll see every caption track the video has, each labelled as uploaded subtitles or auto-generated. If you uploaded your own subtitles, pick that track; it will be more accurate than YouTube's automatic one. Download TXT for writing. Keep the SRT too if you want timestamps later for chapters or for linking to specific moments.

The TXT file has one caption per line, and it keeps the line breaks that were inside each caption. For example, the first lines of "Me at the zoo" come out like this:

All right, so here we are, in front of the
elephants
the cool thing about these guys is that they
have really...

So the first job in your editor is joining lines into sentences and paragraphs. A plain text editor with find-and-replace is enough for this. If you only want to read the text in the browser, the RAW button opens it in a new tab.

Step 2: Decide what kind of post it is

Different videos turn into different articles, and deciding early saves rewriting later. A tutorial becomes a how-to with numbered steps, exact settings and screenshots. An opinion video or explainer becomes an essay with the main point stated at the top and one heading per argument. An interview works best as a Q&A or a highlights post with edited answers. A review should lead with the verdict and put the details below it.

Step 3: Find the structure

Spoken explanations wander, repeat and backtrack. That's fine on video, where your tone carries the listener. On a page it reads as rambling. Read the transcript once without editing and list the main points in the order a reader needs them, which is not always the order you said them. Those points become your headings.

While you read, mark two things: the best example for each point, and every phrase that only makes sense on screen, like "as you can see here" or "click this button". Each of those needs a screenshot or a precise description in the article.

Step 4: Rewrite, don't paste

A cleaned-up transcript is still a transcript. Edit for reading:

  • Cut filler, false starts, repetition and channel housekeeping such as reminders to subscribe.
  • Turn spoken step-by-step instructions into numbered lists, and spoken comparisons into a table.
  • Break up long spoken sentences. What sounds natural aloud often doesn't survive on the page.
  • Fix names, numbers and technical terms that automatic captions got wrong.
  • Move the conclusion up. A video builds toward its point; a reader wants it in the first paragraph.

Step 5: Add what the video didn't have

The post should be worth reading even for someone who has watched the video. Add links to the sources and tools you mention, exact version numbers and settings, code people can copy, corrections to anything you got wrong on camera, and answers to questions viewers asked in the comments. This is the part that makes the article more than a duplicate of the video, for readers and for search engines.

Step 6 (optional): Use an AI assistant for a first draft

Assistants such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or NotebookLM can work from a transcript you paste or upload. Narrow prompts work better than "write a blog post from this":

  • "Outline this transcript as a how-to article. Keep the speaker's steps in order and keep every specific setting."
  • "List every tool, product, number and link mentioned, with the sentence it appears in."
  • "Which parts of this transcript only make sense while watching the video?"
  • "Write a three-sentence summary for the top of the article."

Long transcripts may need to be split; our transcript for ChatGPT page has tips. Then check every fact in the draft against the video and rewrite it in your own voice. AI drafts tend to smooth away exactly the specifics that make your video useful, and they occasionally invent details you never said. Publishing a lightly edited AI summary of your video gives you a generic page, which is the thing you were trying to avoid.

Step 7: Publish alongside the video

Embed the video near the top so readers can switch to watching. Link the post from the video description and the video from the post. Write the post's title for readers searching in text; it doesn't have to match the video title, and often shouldn't.

You can also go the other way and use the post's headings as chapters in the video description. The start times are in the SRT file. YouTube's Video Chapters rules are short: the first timestamp must be 00:00, you need at least three, and each chapter has to be at least 10 seconds long. The result is a video and an article that share the same structure.

Before you publish

Read the post as someone who will never watch the video. Does it make sense on its own? Does it contain at least one useful thing the video didn't? Are all the names and numbers right? If any answer is no, it needs another pass.
