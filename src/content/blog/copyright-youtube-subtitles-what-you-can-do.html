I'm a software developer, not a lawyer, and this is not legal advice. Copyright law differs from country to country. If real money or a real dispute depends on the answer, talk to a lawyer where you live.

The words in a YouTube video's subtitles belong to whoever owns the video, not to YouTube and not to you because you downloaded them. Reading them, studying them and quoting short parts with credit is usually fine. Republishing a full transcript, selling subtitle files or uploading a translation of someone else's video generally needs the owner's permission. The rest of this article explains where those lines come from.

Who owns a transcript

A script, a lecture or an unscripted conversation is protected by copyright once it's recorded, and the protection covers the words, not just the picture. A subtitle file is a written copy of those words with timing attached. That holds whether the creator typed the captions or YouTube's speech recognition produced them: an automatic track is a machine's transcription of the creator's speech, and it doesn't give anyone new rights over the text.

Two things copyright does not protect are worth knowing. Ideas and facts are free: you can explain what a video taught you in your own words. And in the United States, works created by federal government employees as part of their job, such as many NASA videos, aren't protected by copyright at all under 17 U.S.C. § 105. Be careful with that one, though: a news channel's upload of a NASA briefing can include the channel's own commentary, which is theirs.

YouTube's terms are a separate layer

Copyright is about the content. YouTube's Terms of Service are a contract about how you use the platform, and they apply on top. They restrict downloading any part of the service or its content unless YouTube provides a way to do it, YouTube or the rights holders give written permission, or the law allows it. They also prohibit automated access such as scrapers without written permission.

I'll be straight about what that means for a tool like mine. FetchSubs fetches caption tracks that YouTube already shows publicly with each video, one video at a time, at the user's request. It doesn't download the video or audio, and it doesn't store anything. But it doesn't change what you're allowed to do with the text. Our terms of use ask you to use it lawfully and in line with YouTube's terms, and the responsibility for how a transcript is used sits with the person using it.

Fair use, and why there's no safe word count

In the US, the main defence for using copyrighted material without permission is fair use. The US Copyright Office describes the four factors courts weigh:

  1. The purpose and character of your use. Transformative uses, such as criticism, commentary or analysis that adds something new, weigh in your favour, and so does non-profit education.
  2. The nature of the original. Factual works get less protection than highly creative ones.
  3. How much you use, and how important that part is. A short passage that is the heart of the work can still count against you.
  4. The effect on the market for the original. If your copy could replace the original or a licence the owner could sell, that weighs against you.

The Copyright Office is explicit that courts decide case by case and that there is no fixed percentage or number of words you can safely use. YouTube's own fair use page adds a few myths worth dropping: giving credit doesn't by itself make a copy fair, there are no magic words, and writing "no infringement intended" doesn't protect you.

Outside the US, most countries don't have a general fair use rule. They have a list of specific exceptions instead, such as quotation for criticism or review, teaching, or private study, each with its own conditions. The direction is similar, but the details are narrower, which is another reason not to treat this article as the last word.

Common uses, roughly sorted

Usually fine

  • Reading a transcript instead of watching, or alongside the video, for yourself.
  • Taking study notes, searching a long lecture for the part you need, or learning a language from the subtitles.
  • Quoting a sentence or two in a review, article or paper, with the creator named and the video linked, where you're commenting on what was said.
  • Anything at all with captions of your own videos.

Grey area: think about the four factors

  • Longer quotes in commentary or criticism. The more you quote and the less you add, the weaker your case.
  • Using transcripts as data in research. Analysis is transformative, but publishing the full transcripts as supplementary material is a separate step. There's more on this in using YouTube transcripts for qualitative research.
  • Classroom handouts. Education helps, but a full transcript handed out in place of the video is closer to a copy.

Needs permission

  • Publishing a full transcript of someone else's video on your website or blog, even with credit. Credit is good manners; it isn't a licence.
  • Selling subtitle files, or offering them as part of a paid product.
  • Translating someone else's video and publishing the translated subtitles. A translation is a derivative work, which is one of the rights the owner controls.
  • Collecting transcripts at scale to build a public archive or an AI training set. That runs into YouTube's terms as well as copyright; see training AI on YouTube transcripts.

Asking permission is easier than it sounds

Many creators answer a short, specific request: which video, what you want to do with the transcript, where it will appear, and that you'll link back. Keep the reply. Some videos are also published under a Creative Commons licence, which YouTube lets uploaders choose, and that licence states what reuse is allowed and how to credit it. Check the video's description area for it before assuming.

If you're a rights holder

If someone republishes your transcript without permission, you can use the website host's copyright complaint process, or YouTube's process if it's on YouTube.
