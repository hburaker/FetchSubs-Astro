The best use of YouTube transcripts in an online course is with your own videos. If you already teach on YouTube, the captions of those videos are a first draft of your lesson notes, handouts, glossary and quiz questions, and you own all of it. Other people's videos are a different matter: you can watch and read them for research, and you can point students to them, but copying their transcripts into a course you charge for is not OK without permission. This post covers both sides.

Start with your own videos

Paste the link to one of your videos into FetchSubs. You'll see every caption track it has, labelled as either uploaded by you or auto-generated by YouTube. Download what you need:

  • TXT for editing into written material. It's just the spoken text, line by line, without timestamps.
  • SRT or VTT if your course platform lets you upload caption files for the lesson videos. SRT is the older, widely supported format; VTT (WebVTT) is the web standard used by HTML5 video players. Check your platform's help pages for which it accepts. Our SRT vs VTT vs TXT comparison explains the differences.

For scale: a 15-minute talk at a normal speaking pace is around 2,300 words of transcript (that's what I got from the creator track of a 15-minute university commencement speech). So a ten-minute lesson gives you roughly 1,500 words to work with, which is already a decent handout once it's tidied up.

Clean up automatic captions before students see them

If you never uploaded your own subtitles, the only English track will be YouTube's automatic one. It's usable for drafting, but don't hand it out as is. In the automatic tracks I've downloaded for testing, the text is mostly lowercase with almost no punctuation, filler words like "uh" are kept, and markers such as [Music] and [Applause] appear in the text. Technical terms and names are where the mistakes cluster, which in a course is exactly the vocabulary that matters most.

A practical order of work: fix the terms first (search the TXT for each word on your glossary and check its spelling), then add sentence breaks and paragraphs, then cut the spoken-language padding ("so, basically, what we're going to do now is...") that reads badly on the page. If you fix the SRT version too and upload it back to your YouTube video, your YouTube viewers get better captions as well. See YouTube's built-in subtitle editor for that step.

What to make from a transcript

  • Lesson notes. A cleaned transcript with headings is a reading version of the lesson for students who prefer text, are on a slow connection, or are reviewing before an assessment.
  • A glossary. Search the transcript for the terms you define out loud and collect your own definitions in one place.
  • Quiz questions. Write questions in the order the lesson covers the material, so they double as a study guide.
  • A searchable index. With SRT files for each lesson you can tell students where a topic is covered ("Lesson 4, from 06:30"), which saves them scrubbing through video.

An AI assistant can draft any of these from a TXT transcript in a minute or two; our transcript for ChatGPT page covers how to prepare the text. Treat the result as a draft. Assistants sometimes write quiz questions about things you never said, or give an answer key that is plausible and wrong. Every question that reaches a paying student should have been checked by you.

Using other creators' videos

For research: fine

Reading the transcripts of other people's videos while planning a course is no different from reading their books. It's a fast way to see how others sequence a topic, which examples they use, and which questions keep coming up in their comments. Use that to check your outline for gaps, then write your own material. Following someone's structure, examples and wording closely enough that the result is recognisably theirs is copying, whether or not you changed a few words.

Pointing students to a video: usually fine

Linking to a public YouTube video as recommended viewing is normal practice, and it sends the viewer to the creator. Embedding uses YouTube's own player, and creators can turn embedding off for their videos. If your course is paid, it's worth reading YouTube's terms and asking the creator before you build a lesson around someone else's embedded video.

Putting their transcript in a paid course: not without permission

The words spoken in a video belong to whoever made it, and a transcript is a copy of those words. Including the full transcript of someone else's video in course materials you sell is redistributing their work for profit. Crediting them doesn't change that. You need one of these:

  • Written permission from the creator. Explain what the course is, what you'd include, and how you'll credit them. Some creators will say yes, some will ask for payment, some will say no. All three are their call.
  • An open licence. YouTube lets uploaders mark a video with the Creative Commons Attribution (CC BY) licence, which allows reuse, including commercial reuse, as long as you give proper credit. Check the licence on the video itself, not a channel's general reputation.
  • A short, attributed quote used to make your own point, of the kind fair use or fair dealing might cover. This is about quoting a sentence or two in context, not reproducing whole sections, and the rules differ by country.

I'm a developer, not a lawyer, so take this as a plain summary rather than legal advice. Our post on copyright and YouTube subtitles goes into more detail.

Limits

FetchSubs downloads caption tracks that already exist on YouTube. It doesn't transcribe audio, so a video with no captions has nothing to download, and that includes your own video if YouTube hasn't generated captions for it yet. It works one video at a time, so for a course with forty lessons you'll be pasting forty links. And a transcript only covers speech: anything you show on screen without saying it, such as code, a diagram or a spreadsheet, needs its own written explanation in the notes.

If your course is for a school or university audience, subtitles for students and educators has more classroom-specific ideas.
