If your organisation publishes video for marketing, training, teaching or public information, captions are very likely expected of you, and in many cases required. Almost every rule points to the same technical standard, WCAG, at Level AA. In practice that means accurate captions for pre-recorded and live video, and a way for blind viewers to get information that is only shown on screen. This article sets out what the main US and EU rules say about video, with links to the official sources, and ends with a practical plan.

This is general information, not legal advice. The facts below were checked against official sources on 22 November 2026, and several of these rules are still moving. For your own obligations, talk to a lawyer who works in your jurisdiction.

WCAG: the standard the laws point to

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines are published by the W3C. The current version is WCAG 2.2, which became a W3C Recommendation in October 2023, but many laws still reference 2.0 or 2.1. For video this matters less than you might expect: the success criteria for audio and video (the 1.2.x group) are the same in all three versions.

  • 1.2.1 Audio-only and Video-only (Prerecorded), Level A. A transcript for audio-only content such as a podcast; a text or audio alternative for video without sound.
  • 1.2.2 Captions (Prerecorded), Level A. Captions for all pre-recorded audio in synchronised media. W3C's Understanding 1.2.2 defines captions as covering speech and non-speech audio, including who is speaking, and accepts both open (burned-in) and closed captions.
  • 1.2.3 Audio Description or Media Alternative (Prerecorded), Level A. At Level A, a full text alternative (a transcript that includes the visual information) is enough.
  • 1.2.4 Captions (Live), Level AA. Captions for live video such as webinars and live streams.
  • 1.2.5 Audio Description (Prerecorded), Level AA. At AA, the text alternative from 1.2.3 is no longer enough on its own; important visual information has to be available as audio description.
  • Level AAA adds sign language (1.2.6), extended audio description (1.2.7), a media alternative for all pre-recorded video (1.2.8) and an alternative for live audio-only content (1.2.9).

So the usual target, Level AA, means captions on all pre-recorded and live video, plus audio description for pre-recorded video wherever important information appears only on screen. A full text transcript of a video is formally only required at AAA, but once you have accurate captions it costs almost nothing to publish one, and it helps deafblind users who read with a braille display.

One point that catches organisations out: the W3C's captions guidance says automatically generated captions don't meet accessibility requirements unless they are fully accurate. YouTube's automatic captions are a useful starting point, not a finished deliverable.

United States

ADA Title II: state and local governments

In April 2024 the Department of Justice issued a rule making WCAG 2.1 Level AA the technical standard for the websites and mobile apps of state and local governments, including public schools, universities and courts. An interim final rule published on 20 April 2026 pushed the compliance dates back by a year. According to ADA.gov's fact sheet on the web rule, the dates are now:

  • 26 April 2027 for public entities with a total population of 50,000 or more;
  • 26 April 2028 for those under 50,000 and for special district governments.

The delay only moves the deadlines for the technical standard. The ADA's general duties of non-discrimination and effective communication still apply in the meantime, and the DOJ has said it may propose further changes to the rule, so check the fact sheet for the current position before you plan around these dates.

ADA Title III: businesses open to the public

There is no Title III regulation with a technical web standard. The DOJ's 2022 web accessibility guidance says the ADA applies to the goods and services businesses offer online, and lists "no captions on videos" among the barriers for people with hearing disabilities. It describes accessible video as having "synchronized captions that are accurate and identify any speakers in the video." Website accessibility lawsuits against businesses are common, and WCAG Level AA is the benchmark usually used in settlements.

Section 508 and Section 504

Section 508 of the Rehabilitation Act applies to federal agencies and the technology they build, buy and use. According to Section508.gov, the Revised 508 Standards incorporate the WCAG 2.0 Level AA success criteria by reference. If you sell video or e-learning to a federal agency, expect captions and audio description to be part of the contract. Section 504 separately covers organisations that receive federal funding, and some funding agencies have issued their own web accessibility rules with their own timelines.

Broadcast video online

If you are a broadcaster or TV distributor, there is an extra layer. Under the 2010 Twenty-First Century Communications and Video Accessibility Act, the FCC's IP captioning rules require programmes shown on US television with captions to keep those captions when they are delivered online.

European Union

The European Accessibility Act

The European Accessibility Act (Directive (EU) 2019/882) has applied since 28 June 2025, through each member state's national law. It doesn't cover every website. It covers specific products and services, which the European Commission lists as including e-commerce, banking services, e-books, passenger transport services, telephony, and services giving access to audiovisual media. Microenterprises that provide services are exempt from its accessibility requirements, though not when they sell covered products.

For video, the Act works in two ways:

  • If you run an in-scope service, such as an online shop or a banking app, its website and apps must meet the Act's accessibility requirements. Video on those sites is expected to be accessible in the same way as everything else, which in practice means the WCAG caption and audio description criteria.
  • If you provide access to audiovisual media, such as a streaming app or a TV platform, you must make sure access services like subtitles for the deaf and hard of hearing and audio description are passed through and displayed properly. How much programming actually has to be captioned is set separately, under the Audiovisual Media Services Directive and national media law.

The technical yardstick in Europe is the harmonised standard EN 301 549, which includes the WCAG criteria for web content. A new version, v4.1.1, written with the Act in mind and based on WCAG 2.2, was published on 2 September 2026. Ireland's National Disability Authority explains that it only becomes the formal benchmark once the European Commission cites it in the Official Journal, expected in December 2026; until then v3.2.1, based on WCAG 2.1, remains the version in force. If you are starting work now, aiming at WCAG 2.2 AA means you won't have to redo it.

The Web Accessibility Directive (public sector)

EU public sector bodies have been covered for longer by the Web Accessibility Directive (EU) 2016/2102. Two exclusions matter for video: live audio and video are out of its scope, and so is pre-recorded audio and video published before 23 September 2020. Pre-recorded video published after that date is in scope, which in practice means captions.

Other countries have their own rules, and most of them also reference WCAG. If you operate in several markets, Level AA of the latest WCAG version is the safest single target.

A workable plan for video

  1. Make an inventory. List every video on your website, apps and channels, with its owner, its publication date and whether it has captions, audio description and a transcript. The publication date matters for the EU public sector exemption, and the owner is who you will chase.
  2. Prioritise. Start with videos people need to use a service (how to apply, how to pay, safety information), then high-traffic videos, then the archive.
  3. Fix captions on videos already on YouTube. Download the existing track as an SRT file with FetchSubs, correct names, numbers and terminology, add speaker and sound labels, and upload the corrected file in YouTube Studio. Our caption checklist covers what to check. FetchSubs only downloads tracks that exist; it does not transcribe audio, so a video with no track at all needs a transcription service or a human captioner.
  4. Check for visual-only information. Charts, on-screen text and demonstrations that the narration never mentions need audio description. For new videos, the cheap fix is to write the description into the script.
  5. Publish a transcript. Once the corrected captions are on YouTube, the same text is available as a plain text transcript you can put below the player or link to.
  6. Plan live captions in advance. YouTube's automatic live captions are English only and have other limits, so for webinars and public meetings, book a human captioner or a service you have tested.
  7. Write it into contracts. If agencies or freelancers make your videos, make accurate captions and, where needed, audio description part of the deliverable rather than something you fix afterwards.

Why it's worth doing anyway

The World Health Organization estimates that about 430 million people need rehabilitation for disabling hearing loss, and captions also serve people watching on mute or in a second language. To see how deaf and hard-of-hearing people actually use captions, read this viewer's guide. And if you're deciding whether to burn captions into a video or provide them as a track, both satisfy WCAG, but they behave very differently; see open vs closed captions.
