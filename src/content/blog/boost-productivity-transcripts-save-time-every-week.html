Reading a transcript of a work video is usually faster than watching it, but the reason isn't the one you often hear. Reading isn't three times faster than listening. At average speeds it takes roughly a third less time, about the same as playing the video at 1.5x. The bigger saving comes from what text lets you do that video doesn't: skim, search, skip and quote. This post goes through the numbers and then the habits that actually save time.

The numbers: reading vs. watching

A widely repeated claim says people speak at about 150 words per minute and read at 250 to 300, so reading a transcript takes a third of the time. Half of that holds up.

Reading speed. The most thorough review I know of is Marc Brysbaert's meta-analysis of 190 studies, How many words do we read per minute? (Journal of Memory and Language, 2019). It puts the average silent reading rate of English-speaking adults at about 238 words per minute for non-fiction and 260 for fiction, and argues that the popular 300 wpm figure is too high. Individuals vary a lot around that average.

Speaking speed. Rather than trust a rule of thumb, I measured two well-known talks using their creator-uploaded English captions from FetchSubs:

  • Steve Jobs' 2005 Stanford commencement address: about 2,300 words over 14.9 minutes of captions, roughly 154 words per minute.
  • Randy Pausch's "Last Lecture" at Carnegie Mellon: about 12,600 words over 76.5 minutes, roughly 164 words per minute.

Those rates include pauses, applause and laughter, so they're what you actually sit through. At 238 wpm, the Stanford speech takes under 10 minutes to read instead of 15, and the Pausch lecture about 53 minutes instead of 76. A real saving, but not a threefold one.

Playback speed. There's also the option of just speeding the video up. A study by Kuperman, Brysbaert and colleagues (2021) found that listening comprehension held up well when speech was played at 270 words per minute and dropped sharply at 315, and that the most efficient listening rate was very close to the same people's reading rate. Their conclusion was that listening and reading follow similar time limits. In practice, a 160 wpm lecture at 1.5x runs at about 240 wpm, which is close to average reading speed.

So if your plan is to consume every word from start to finish, reading and fast playback are about even. Pick whichever you concentrate better with.

Where transcripts really save time

Most work videos don't need every word. You need to know whether a 50-minute webinar has anything for you, find the one setting someone mentioned in a conference talk, or check exactly what was said before you repeat it in a report. That's where text wins by a wide margin, because video makes you go through it in order and text doesn't.

Triage before you commit

Open the transcript and spend two minutes skimming the start, the end, and a few points in between. You'll know whether the talk covers what the title promised. Plenty of long videos turn out to have five useful minutes, and some have none. Deciding that in two minutes instead of twenty is the biggest single saving in this post.

Search instead of scrubbing

If you're looking for something specific, such as a library name, a figure, or the part about pricing, press Ctrl+F (Cmd+F on a Mac) in the transcript. With the SRT version, each hit comes with a timestamp, so if you do need to see the slide or the demo, you can jump straight to that moment instead of dragging the playhead around.

Quote accurately

When you summarise a talk for colleagues, a copied sentence from the transcript is more reliable than your memory of what was said, and the timestamp lets anyone check it. If the track is auto-generated, listen to that passage once before quoting it; automatic captions are weakest on names, numbers and jargon.

Feed it to an AI assistant, carefully

Paste a TXT transcript into ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini and ask specific questions: "Which tools does the speaker recommend, and for what?" or "What does she say about migration from version 2?" Specific questions get more useful answers than "summarise this". The assistant can still misread or invent details, so check anything you're going to act on against the transcript itself. Our guides on preparing a transcript for ChatGPT and summarising YouTube videos with ChatGPT or Claude go into more detail.

A workflow that fits into a working day

  1. When a work-related video lands in your queue, paste its link into FetchSubs.
  2. If there's a creator-uploaded track, pick it; otherwise use the auto-generated one. FetchSubs labels which is which.
  3. Click RAW to read the text in a new browser tab, or download TXT if you want to keep it. Take SRT if you expect to need timestamps.
  4. Skim, search, decide. Watch only the parts where the picture matters.
  5. Paste the two or three things worth keeping into wherever you keep work notes, with the video link.

If you want to remember what you read for months rather than days, that's a different job; building a personal knowledge library from YouTube describes a note-and-review system for it.

When watching is the better choice

  • The content is visual. Software demos, code on screen, design reviews and anything with charts lose most of their meaning as text. A transcript of "and then you click here" is no use.
  • Tone matters. An interview where you want to judge how confident someone sounds, or a product announcement where the audience reaction matters, is better watched.
  • There are no captions. FetchSubs downloads captions that already exist on YouTube; it doesn't transcribe audio. If a video has no track, there's nothing to read. See why a transcript might not be available.
  • The automatic captions are poor. Strong accents, crosstalk and specialist vocabulary can make an auto-generated transcript slower to decode than the audio.

A note on "learning styles"

You'll sometimes see transcripts recommended because some people are "read/write learners", following models like VARK. I'd leave that argument out. A review by Pashler and colleagues (2008) found no adequate evidence that matching teaching to a person's preferred learning style improves results. The case for transcripts doesn't need it. Text is searchable and skimmable, and video isn't. That's enough.
