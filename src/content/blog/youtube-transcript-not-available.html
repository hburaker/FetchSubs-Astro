--- title: "YouTube Transcript Not Available? Why It Happens and What to Do" summary: "No Show transcript button or an empty caption list? The real reasons a YouTube video has no transcript, and what you can do about each one." author: "Halit Burak Eroğlu" publishDate: "2026-11-22" lang: "en" order: 31 ---

A YouTube transcript is the video's caption track shown as a list. If the video has no captions, neither uploaded by the creator nor generated by YouTube, there's nothing to show, and no website or app that reads YouTube captions can conjure one up. The usual causes: the creator removed captions or never published any, the video is too new, the language isn't supported, or the audio defeated speech recognition. Sometimes, though, the captions are there and only the button is missing. The first job is to work out which of the two you're dealing with.

Missing transcript, or missing button?

  1. Check for captions in the player. On a computer, the CC icon at the bottom right of the player only appears when captions exist. The gear menu's Subtitles/CC entry lists every track, for example English or English (auto-generated).
  2. Look for Show transcript. On desktop, expand the description (...more) and scroll down. YouTube's help page on viewing video transcripts says transcripts are available for videos that have captions. The layout shifts now and then, and the mobile website in particular may not offer the button even where the app does.
  3. Ask a caption reader. Paste the link into the FetchSubs YouTube transcript tool. It lists every caption track YouTube serves for the video, each labelled (Official) or (Auto-generated). An empty list means YouTube really has no captions for it.

If the CC menu shows languages but there's no Show transcript, the captions exist and the problem is the interface. You can still get the text: export the track as TXT, or click RAW to open it in a new browser tab. If there's no CC icon and the track list is empty, keep reading.

Why a video has no captions

YouTube's help page Use automatic captioning lists the reasons automatic captions may not be generated. Add the choices creators make in YouTube Studio, and you have nearly every case.

The creator removed captions, or never published any

YouTube publishes automatic captions by default when it can create them, but the creator has the final say. In YouTube Studio they can edit or remove caption tracks, the automatic one included, and nothing obliges them to upload a replacement.

What you can do: ask. A short, polite comment or email saying you rely on captions, for accessibility, study or translation, costs nothing. The creator may not know the track is gone.

The video is too new

Automatic captions aren't created at the moment of upload. YouTube says they may not be available yet when the audio is complex and still being processed, and it gives no fixed waiting time. A video published an hour ago may simply not have its track yet. Check again after a few hours, or the next day, before you decide it never will.

The language isn't supported

Automatic captions only cover the languages on YouTube's list, which has 67 entries at the time of writing, from Afrikaans to Zulu. Speech in any other language gets no automatic track. The same help page has two limits that are easy to miss. If a video has several audio tracks in different languages, automatic captions are made for the default one only. And videos where several languages are spoken at the same time may not be captioned at all.

Look through the whole Subtitles/CC list, not just your own language. A creator may have uploaded subtitles in one language even though automatic captions failed for another. YouTube's player can auto-translate an existing track if you just need to follow along, but there has to be a track to start from.

The audio is hard to recognise

YouTube names poor sound quality or speech it doesn't recognise, speakers talking over each other, and a long silence at the start of the video. The last one is worth knowing if you make videos: a long silent or music-only intro can be enough to cause trouble. Purely instrumental, ambient or silent videos have no speech to transcribe, so an empty track there is expected. As a viewer there's little you can do beyond asking the creator, or transcribing the audio yourself (see the end of this article).

The video is very long

"The video is too long" is on YouTube's own list. It doesn't say where the cut-off is, so assume multi-hour recordings, such as a full conference day or a long live-stream archive, are the likeliest to be affected. Conferences and podcasts often upload individual talks or highlight clips separately, and those shorter uploads have a much better chance of carrying captions.

Live streams and recent premieres

Live captions follow different rules. According to YouTube, live automatic captions are English only, work only on normal-latency streams, and don't stay on the video once the stream ends. The archive gets fresh captions through the normal processing for uploads, and those can differ from what viewers saw live. So a stream that has just finished may show no transcript for a while. If a premiere or archive still has nothing after a day or two, length, language or audio is the likely reason.

Private, members-only, age-restricted or region-locked videos

Captions belong to the video and share its access rules. Outside tools can't read private or members-only videos at all, and age-restricted or region-blocked videos may fail depending on where and how the request is made. If you have access, YouTube's own transcript panel, used while signed in, is the way in.

Shorts

Shorts can have caption tracks; YouTube's help page covers automatic captions on long-form videos and Shorts alike. The Shorts player just has fewer controls, so the transcript is harder to reach. Replace /shorts/ in the address with /watch?v= to open the Short in the normal player, or paste the Shorts link straight into FetchSubs, which accepts it. Plenty of Shorts are mostly music or on-screen text, so an empty list is common.

Same speech, different uploads

Captions belong to a specific upload, not to the content. I looked up two YouTube copies of President Kennedy's 1962 speech at Rice University with FetchSubs. The NASA Video channel's upload has two English tracks; a full-length re-upload by someone else has none:

President Kennedy's Speech at Rice University (NASA Video, 18:15)
  English (auto-generated)   auto-generated
  English - Caption          official

JFK - We choose to go to the Moon, full length (re-upload, 17:48)
  (no caption tracks)

From the outside there's no telling why the second copy has nothing. Maybe the uploader removed the captions, maybe the audio hit one of the limits above. The useful point is that another upload of the same talk, interview or lecture may well have a transcript, and official channels (the organisation that hosted the event, or the speaker's own channel) are the first place to look.

When there really is no transcript

Once you're sure the video has no caption tracks, these are the options, roughly in the order I'd try them:

  1. Wait, if the video, premiere or stream is less than a day old.
  2. Check every language in the Subtitles/CC menu or the FetchSubs track list, not just your own.
  3. Find another source. The official upload, a shorter clip, or the same material as a podcast episode, article or slide deck. Many conferences, universities and podcasts publish transcripts on their own websites.
  4. Ask the creator. They can restore, publish or upload captions in YouTube Studio, which helps every viewer, not just you.
  5. If it's your own video, open YouTube Studio, go to Subtitles, and see whether an automatic track exists or was deleted. If YouTube couldn't make one, you can upload a caption file, type captions, or paste a transcript and let YouTube sync it.
  6. Transcribe it yourself. Speech-to-text software can make a transcript from an audio file. Whisper is a widely used open-source model that runs on your own computer, so the audio never leaves it. It takes some setup and needs a copy of the audio. Only do this with a file you're entitled to use: your own recording, one the creator gave you, or one published under a licence that allows it. YouTube's terms don't allow downloading videos except where YouTube itself offers a download. And proofread the result; machine transcripts always need it.

What FetchSubs can and can't do here

FetchSubs reads the caption tracks YouTube already has and lets you save them. It takes watch, youtu.be, Shorts and embed links, it's free and there's no sign-up. Every track is listed with its language and marked (Official) or (Auto-generated), and each row has its own buttons, so when a video has both kinds in one language you download the one you click. Formats are SRT, VTT and TXT, plus RAW for the clean text in a new tab. If you want a plain document rather than timed subtitles, the YouTube to text page explains the TXT export.

It doesn't transcribe audio, translate or summarise. If YouTube has no captions for a video, FetchSubs can't create them, and neither can any other tool that works from YouTube's captions. If the automatic track is all there is, it helps to know where it tends to slip: how accurate YouTube auto-generated subtitles are compares an automatic and an official track of the Kennedy speech line by line.
