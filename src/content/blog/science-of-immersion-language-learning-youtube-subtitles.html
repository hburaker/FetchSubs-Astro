If you're learning a language from YouTube, watch with subtitles in the language you're learning, not in your own. That's the one piece of advice here with decent research behind it. The rest of this post explains what that research did and didn't show, and how to turn a downloaded transcript into actual practice instead of background viewing.

What the studies tested

Same-language subtitles help; translated ones can get in the way

The study people usually cite is by Holger Mitterer and James McQueen, published in PLoS ONE in 2009. Dutch university students who were good at English but unfamiliar with Scottish and Australian accents watched about 25 minutes of a Scottish film or an Australian sitcom, either with English subtitles, Dutch subtitles, or none. Afterwards they heard audio-only phrases in that accent and repeated them back.

On phrases they had never heard before, the group that watched with English subtitles did better than the group with no subtitles. The group that watched with Dutch subtitles did worse. The authors' explanation is that subtitles in the spoken language tell you which words you're hearing, so you learn how those words sound in that accent; subtitles in your own language give you the meaning directly, so you stop working on the sound.

Keep the scope in mind. These were advanced learners adapting to an unfamiliar accent, in groups of 20, after one short viewing. It's a good reason to prefer target-language subtitles. It isn't proof that subtitled YouTube alone will take a beginner to fluency.

Captions and vocabulary

A broader picture comes from a meta-analysis by Maribel Montero Perez and colleagues (System, 2013), which pooled studies comparing captioned and uncaptioned video for language learners. It found a large benefit of captions for listening comprehension and for vocabulary learning. That fits the everyday experience of seeing a word written while you hear it: you can look it up, and you're more likely to recognise it the next time it comes past without text.

Comprehensible input

The linguist Stephen Krashen popularised the idea that we acquire a language by understanding messages slightly above our current level, which he called comprehensible input. How far that theory goes is still argued about. The practical version is hard to argue with: pick videos you can mostly follow, and use the text to bridge the parts you can't. If you need the subtitles for every single sentence, the video is probably too hard for now.

Choosing the right track

Paste a video link into FetchSubs and you'll see every caption track the video has, each labelled as the creator's own subtitles or as auto-generated. Two things to check:

Which track matches the audio? When I loaded Steve Jobs' 2005 Stanford commencement speech, it listed creator-uploaded tracks in eight languages, including Arabic, Italian, Japanese and two kinds of Spanish. Only the English one matches what you hear; the others are translations. For listening practice, the track in the spoken language is the one you want. A translated track is your native-language crutch, useful for checking meaning, not for training your ear.

Creator track or automatic? Prefer the creator's track when one exists. Here is the same moment from the Stanford speech in both versions:

Creator track:  Thank You. I am honored to be with you today at your commencement
Auto-generated: thank
                you I'm uh honored to be with you today
                for your commencement

The automatic track is lowercase, has almost no punctuation across the whole 15-minute speech, keeps the filler "uh", and adds markers like [Music] and [Applause]. It also disagrees with the creator track on small words ("at your" versus "for your", "I am" versus "I'm"). Only the audio can settle which is right, and that kind of mismatch makes a good listening exercise in itself. More on how reliable automatic captions are in our accuracy guide.

Automatic captions do have one advantage for learners: they tend to keep fillers and contractions that a tidied-up creator track removes, which is closer to how people actually talk. Just don't memorise a phrase that looks strange without checking it.

Exercises with a downloaded transcript

Watching is passive. Working with the text is where most of the practice happens. Download the target-language track as plain text (or SRT if you want timestamps), then try these:

  1. Pre-read, then watch. Skim the transcript and look up the five to ten words that block your understanding. Then watch the video straight through without pausing.
  2. Dictation. Play one sentence, write down what you hear, and compare with the transcript. The SRT file tells you exactly where each line starts, so it's easy to loop a short section.
  3. Shadowing. Play a sentence, pause, and say it aloud while reading it, copying the rhythm and intonation. Short sections, many repeats.
  4. Sentence cards. Copy whole sentences, not single words, into a spaced-repetition app such as Anki (free on desktop and Android; the iOS app is paid). A word inside a real sentence is easier to remember and to use correctly.

Twenty minutes of this on a five-minute clip is worth more than an hour of watching with subtitles on in the background.

Two languages side by side

If a video has tracks in both your target language and your own, you can download both and read them in parallel when you get stuck. Many videos have only one track, and the two rarely line up sentence by sentence, because translators merge and split lines. Treat the second track as a reference, not as a word-for-word key. If there's no track in your language at all, our guide on translating YouTube subtitles covers the options; FetchSubs itself doesn't translate.

What subtitles won't do

Reading along trains recognition: you understand more of what you hear. It doesn't train you to produce the language, which takes speaking and writing. And subtitles can become a habit you lean on. Once a video feels comfortable with target-language subtitles, watch it again without them and see how much you still follow.

Real speech is also messier than any textbook, with slang, regional accents, jokes and half-finished sentences. That's the point of using YouTube, but it means you'll hit things a dictionary doesn't explain. Keep a short list of those and ask a teacher or a native speaker.
