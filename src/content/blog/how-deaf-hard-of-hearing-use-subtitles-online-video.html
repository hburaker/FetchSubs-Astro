If you are deaf or hard of hearing, two things decide whether a YouTube video works for you: which caption track you're reading, and whether you can read it comfortably. This guide is about the viewer's side of captions: how to tell a reviewed track from a machine one, how to make captions easier to read, how to use the transcript, and what your options are when a video has no captions at all.

If you make videos, the companion piece is our caption checklist for creators.

Check which track you're reading

A YouTube video can carry two kinds of caption track. Some are written or corrected by a person, usually the creator. Others come from YouTube's speech recognition, and in the player's caption menu those show "(auto-generated)" after the language name, for example "English (auto-generated)". A video can have both, and when it does, the human track is almost always the better choice.

Automatic captions are often fine for one clear speaker. They get worse with music, crosstalk, accents the system wasn't trained on, and anything with names or numbers. YouTube's own help page on automatic captions says they may misrepresent the spoken content. So if a line in an automatic track doesn't make sense, it is quite possibly a recognition error rather than what the person said. Our accuracy test shows where those errors tend to land.

If you want to see every track a video has before you start watching, paste the link into FetchSubs. It lists each track with a label: (Official) for subtitles the creator provided, (Auto-generated) for YouTube's machine captions.

Make the captions readable

The default white-on-black captions don't suit everyone. On a computer, click Settings (the gear icon) in the player, then Subtitles/CC, then Options. You can change the font, colour, size and opacity of the text, the background and the caption window, and the edge style. According to YouTube's captions help page, these become your default caption settings until you change them again. The mobile apps handle this differently by platform, and the same help page has tabs with the steps for Android and iPhone.

A larger font with a solid background is easier to read over busy footage, such as a screen recording full of text. A semi-transparent background is a reasonable middle ground if the captions keep covering something you need to see.

Use the transcript

Captions disappear after a few seconds; a transcript stays put. On a computer, open the video's description and click Show transcript. YouTube's transcript help page explains that the panel scrolls along with the video and that clicking a line jumps to that moment. That makes it useful for re-reading a sentence you missed, or for skimming a long talk before you decide to watch it.

The transcript panel only shows what's in the caption track, though, and you can't take it with you. If you want to keep the text, search it, enlarge it with your own reading tools or read it offline, download it. In FetchSubs, paste the link and pick TXT for a plain text file, or RAW to open the clean text in a new browser tab. Here is what the TXT download looks like for "Me at the zoo", the first video ever uploaded to YouTube:

All right, so here we are, in front of the
elephants
the cool thing about these guys is that they
have really...
really really long trunks
and that's cool
(baaaaaaaaaaahhh!!)
and that's pretty much all there is to
say

There are no timestamps, so it reads like notes. If you want the timing too, pick SRT. This works in a phone browser as well; see downloading subtitles on iPhone and Android.

Small habits that help

  • Slow down fast speakers. YouTube's playback speed setting lets you drop to 0.75x. Fast captions get more time on screen, and lip movements are still easy to follow.
  • Read ahead on technical videos. Skimming the transcript first means you already know the unfamiliar terms when they flash past in the captions.
  • Use your own player for saved videos. If you have a video file of your own, a player such as VLC can load an SRT file next to it, and you control the caption size and position there.

When a video has no captions

FetchSubs can only download caption tracks that already exist on YouTube. It doesn't create captions from the audio, so if the video has no tracks at all, there is nothing for it to fetch. In that case your options are device-level captions or asking the creator.

Live Caption in Chrome generates captions for any audio playing in the browser. On Windows, Mac and Linux it's under Chrome's Settings, then Accessibility; on a Chromebook it's in the accessibility settings under Audio and captions. Google's Live Caption help page says the audio and captions are processed on your device and never leave it. English works by default, and other languages need a language pack. Many Android phones have a Live Caption option of their own in the accessibility settings.

Live Captions on Apple devices is under Settings, then Accessibility, then Live Captions. Apple's iPhone guide notes that it isn't available in every language or region, needs a recent device, and that accuracy may vary.

Both are speech recognition, so they share the weaknesses of YouTube's automatic captions, and neither gives you a file you can save. They are a fallback, not a replacement for a proper track.

Asking the creator sometimes works, especially on smaller channels. A short comment saying you rely on captions, ideally with the timestamp of a line that came out wrong, gives them something concrete to fix.

Spotting good and bad captions quickly

Reviewed captions tend to have proper punctuation and capital letters, correct spellings of names and technical terms, a label when the speaker is off-screen, and descriptions of sounds that matter, like [phone buzzing] or [audience laughs]. Unreviewed automatic captions usually give themselves away: long runs without punctuation, no speaker changes marked, and little beyond [Music] for anything that isn't speech.

The zoo video above is a small example of the human side. The line (baaaaaaaaaaahhh!!) isn't speech; someone wrote it in so that a viewer who can't hear knows there's a loud noise. It doesn't say what made the noise, which a more careful caption would, but it's more than speech recognition would have given you.

Live streams

YouTube's automatic captions on live streams are limited. Its help page says they are English only, only for streams at normal latency (not low or ultra-low latency), and still rolling out to channels. Some events hire human captioners instead, which is usually more accurate. Once a stream ends, the archived video normally gets regular automatic captions, which you can read or download like any other video. Our article on live stream captions goes into more detail.

What you can expect from organisations

Individual YouTubers generally aren't legally required to caption. Many organisations are, or are expected to: public bodies, universities, broadcasters and, depending on the country, businesses. In the US, the Department of Justice's web guidance lists missing video captions as an accessibility barrier. Our guide to accessibility law for video summarises the main US and EU rules, if you need to point an organisation to them.
